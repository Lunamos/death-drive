# Finding the story

## Overview

The story is not chosen in advance. It is re-derived from the evidence at every milestone, and it steers the work that remains.
A presentation is right when a reader who knew nothing comes away believing something new and true, moved there by the
strongest evidence.

## At each milestone

1. **The claim.** Write the one-sentence claim the evidence supports now: the explanation you would give someone who knows
   nothing (narrating-work).
2. **The reader's move.** State what a knowledgeable reader believes before reading, and what they should believe after. The
   surprise lives in the distance between the two.
3. **The lead evidence.** Find the single piece of evidence that moves them most. It leads, as the first figure and the first
   paragraph.
4. **The arc.** Choose the arc that makes that move fastest. The arc follows the kind of contribution:

   | Contribution | Arc |
   |---|---|
   | A hidden capability | failure -> minimal change -> gain -> why |
   | A law | pattern -> breadth -> prediction |
   | A puzzle | anomaly -> explanation -> test |
   | An analysis | question -> measurement across systems -> what it settles |
   | A method | problem -> gain -> why it works |
   | A limit | belief -> counterexample -> boundary |
   | An open problem | phenomenon -> why existing accounts fail -> what exactly remains open |

   The arc always closes its loop: the question raised at the start is answered, or left precisely open, at the end.

5. **Claims and gaps.** List each claim of the arc with its evidence. Gaps become the next experiments. Claims the evidence does
   not support are narrowed.
6. **The interest test.** Test the story against the origin with judging-interest. If it is ordinary, look for the reframing
   the evidence allows before writing more.

## Across milestones

- **Keep the latest story** in the project, dated (e.g. `STORY.md`). Earlier versions stay in its history.
- **When the evidence shifts the claim,** re-derive the story instead of patching the old one.
- **When several stories answer one question,** merge them into one complete work.
- **Names follow the reader.** Use words the reader already knows, and rename anything readers misunderstand.

## Leaving room for later work

State what the work makes possible: the questions it opens, the method or measurement others can reuse, and the predictions
others can test. A milestone paper tells the next researcher where to go.

## Selecting the presentation

- **Independent tellings.** They beat incremental edits:
  1. A separate writing model tells the story from scratch (writing-with-a-separate-model).
  2. Blind readers compare the versions.
  3. The user's taste chooses.
- **The final pass.** Before the final version, re-derive the story once more from the complete evidence, and align every number
  and setting with it.
