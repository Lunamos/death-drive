# Finding the question

## Overview

The drive comes from the person. The model can map a field, find its open problems and propose questions. What is worth a long
pursuit is set by the user's curiosity and vision, so draw these out first; they anchor every later judgment.

## Ask the user

Ask one question at a time, follow up on the answers, and ask for concrete examples.

- **The question:** which question in this field do you most want answered? What would change, for you or for the field, if it
  were answered?
- **The vision:** what should the field understand, or be able to do, in a few years that it cannot now?
- **Curiosity:** which open problems keep pulling at you? Which answer would surprise you most?
- **Admired work:** which past work, yours or others', do you admire, and why?
- **History:** what have you already tried or believed, and what did it teach you?
- **Excitement:** what result would excite you enough to drop everything else? What would make you lose interest?
- **Constraints:** what time, resources and limits apply?

## Map the field

While the user answers, survey the field, using subagents for breadth and the available literature-search skills. Cover:
- the settled results;
- the open problems;
- where the evidence is thin or contradictory;
- the recent surprises;
- the user's own prior work.

Bring back a short map, and tie each open problem to the curiosity the user has expressed.

## The origin

Agree on a short origin statement with the user, and keep it in the project (e.g. `ORIGIN.md`). It gives:
- the question, in one sentence;
- why it matters, to the user and to the field;
- what a great answer would look like;
- two or three candidate directions, each with the result that would make it exciting.

Every later judgment is weighed against the origin (judging-interest), and letting go returns to it (the-drive). Candidate
questions come from the map; the choice comes from the user. When the work drifts, or after an idea is let go, revisit the
origin with the user.
